Throughout history, censorship has been a major obstacle to the freedom of expression of people. It was common in ancient societies such as China to use censorship as a means of regulating the morals and politics of their citizens. It can be traced back to Rome in 443 BC when the first censorship office was created. The idea of effective government in ancient Rome was shaped by the character of the people, as it was in ancient Greek communities. Consequently, censorship was respected. (Mette Newth, 2010)

**What is Censorship?**

According to Wikipedia, the suppression of speech, public communication, or other information is called censorship. It may be done because such material is deemed objectionable, harmful, sensitive, or "inconvenient". A government, a private institution, or another controlling body may censor material as well. Some countries censor the Internet to a moderate degree, while others go so far as to prevent citizens from accessing information like news or suppressing debate among citizens. There is also Internet censorship in response to events like elections, protests, and riots. This kind of censorship can be seen in dictatorial countries such as China, North Korea, Pakistan, and Iran. 

**Censorship in Iran**

Since the revolution in 1979, the government of the Islamic Republic of Iran has limited all kinds of media such as radio, television, internet, and press.

There is no freedom of expression or media independence online or offline. State-controlled television remains a major source of information for many Iranians, but reporting and analysis are heavily censored. Opposition members and critics are rarely given a platform. 

**Censorship in news agencies and newspapers**

The government censors and warns public media about which topics they should cover. A large number of foreign-based websites, including news sites and major social media platforms, are filtered. Iran forbids satellite dishes and regularly jams Persian-language broadcasts from abroad. In some protests and political events, journalists including photographers and cameras were arrested.  Journalists working for Persian-language media outside of Iran have been summoned and threatened by Iranian authorities. After the protests in November, this type of intimidation heightened.

**Censorship in cinema**

Revolution in 1979 in Iran affected nearly all aspects of life. The movie industry in Iran is not immune to the effects of the revolution. Across the country, movies are heavily censored to ensure they don't contain sexual content or political references. (Zibari, 2019). The Iranian government seeks to prevent the Iranian population from absorbing Western culture. As a result, Hollywood movies are no longer screened in movie theaters, and Iranian movies tend to take their place.

**Internet block began**

Marks (2021) states that as one of the few countries in the Middle East to have internet access, Iran became the second country in 1993. The number of Iranians using the internet has risen significantly since then. Iranian state regulation was initially light. While internet usage grew in popularity, the Islamic Republic of Iran's religious and judicial authorities began to restrict access to the content they considered "counter-revolutionary", "anti-Islamic" or "anti-social". They have worked to align censorship of the internet with existing restrictions on the media, political affiliations, and religious expression. Based on statistics of Freedom in the World (2020), there have been a total of 886 domains blocked by Iranian ISPs in the past three years. Most of these restrictions affected Western media outlets and human rights organizations' websites. Tens of thousands of similar websites are on a state blacklist. In the government announcement, these websites have been blocked because they produce or display "immoral" or "un-Islamic" content but in fact, their contents were against the regime. 

**What are the censorship measures in Iran?**

Iranian officials invested $36 million in the "smart filtering" technology in 2016 (Marks, 2021). This software was previously developed in China. Authorities would be able to selectively censor citizens' internet access with this software. A large number of websites are permanently blocked by Iranian ISPs, including Twitter, Facebook, YouTube, and WordPress. Moreover, Viber was blocked after it was revealed that it was owned by Israeli citizens. In April 2017, when Telegram introduced free encrypted voice calling, the Attorney General ordered all ISPs to block the feature immediately and permanently. Due to this, citizens cannot access the blocked websites in the country using an internet connection (MacLellan, 2018).

**Finding ways to escape from censorship on the internet**

 Iranian citizens are constantly creating new methods to overcome state censorship despite state-sponsored attempts by the Iranian administration to censor access to the internet. One of the important ways is using VPN.  Many people use VPNs to bypass internet restrictions. Iranian government officials continue to track and block popular VPN providers' IP addresses. Fortunately, VPN service providers are not giving up. For instance, many Iranians access BBC News, YouTube channels, and Twitter through VPNs.  

**Vague future of the internet in Iran**

According to Iran's parliament, the Protection Bill has now been sent to the Guardian Council by February 20, paving the way for the imposition of more restrictions on Iran's Internet than ever before (Carter, 2022). In the new law, all international services will be banned under a blanket ban, which should further tighten the net. It is expected that international providers of online services - such as Google, Microsoft, Amazon, and Facebook, as well as smaller ones - will be required to operate within Islamic Republic laws and collaborate with the state in surveillance and censorship operations. Tech companies may also need to install an official representative inside the country. They will be blocked from Iranians if they do not follow domestic rules.

Bowen (n.d) states that a country-wide, independent network that will host important domestic online services has been discussed intermittently by the government over the past decade. It hosts only "clean" content that is "compatible with revolutionary and religious values." This network will be monitored and censored in its entirety. The world internet will be replaced by the domestic internet in Iran. The Iranian government tends to be a country similar to North Korea. For many Iranians, this will be yet another episode in their decades-long nightmare.
