Before the 1600s, the old plays focused on kings, queens, and other rich and powerful people, and most of the main characters were elite and powerful. These plays shed light on their emotions, their duties, and the tragic consequences of their actions. But in the 1600s, something began to change. Writers in both England and France started creating characters from outside the upper class and portrayed servants, merchants, and middle-class women. This change is clear in Molière's *Tartuffe* and William Congreve's *The Way of the World*, where people from lower social classes are not only present but also important to the plot. These plays demonstrate that, during the 1600s, theatre was starting to reflect real society, where intelligence and power could be found in all classes, not just the rich.

## The Maid Who Sees Through Everyone

In *Tartuffe*, the maid Dorine is a perfect example of how servants gained power on stage. Even though she is a servant with no formal authority, she is smarter and braver than her master, Orgon. While Orgon blindly defends Tartuffe, a man pretending to be religious, Dorine sees through the act from the start. She openly mocks Orgon's devotion to the impostor, contradicts him to his face, and warns the family about the scandal Tartuffe is bringing into the house. Her confidence and quick thinking stand in contrast to Orgon's foolishness. Erika Fischer-Lichte notes how farce challenged the social order by turning servants into voices of reason, and Dorine clearly plays that role. She is not just comic relief: she helps move the plot forward and protects her household from disaster.

## Servants Who Run the Plot

*The Way of the World* also gives major power to servants. Waitwell, a manservant, pretends to be a rich gentleman named Sir Rowland as part of Mirabell's plan to trick Lady Wishfort. His wife, Foible, who is Lady Wishfort's maid, helps him carry out the scheme. These two characters from the lower class shape the entire story. In older plays, servants were often silent or only used for comedy, but here they are key drivers of the plot. Foible knows exactly how to flatter and manage her mistress, and she has real influence over her. The success of their plan shows how well the servants understand the flaws of the upper class. According to Fischer-Lichte, Restoration theatre moved away from heroic tales and toward comedies that reflected everyday struggles and class dynamics (243–244). Foible and Waitwell reflect this shift, giving voice and agency to those who once stood in the background.

## A New Audience

This move toward portraying middle-class life was influenced by real changes in society. During the seventeenth century, the middle class was growing quickly in England and across Europe. With more money and free time, they became an important audience for plays. As Fischer-Lichte puts it, theatre "shifted from being a place where the values of society were discussed to one where it entertained the growing middle class with humorous and often critical stories" (244). These new audiences wanted stories they could relate to, stories that mirrored their own lives, struggles, and values, not just tales about royalty.

## Women Who Take Control

Women also began to appear more strongly in plays from this period. In earlier tragedies, women were often shown as ruled by emotion and doomed by it. In Racine's *Phaedra*, for example, the queen is overwhelmed by a forbidden love and her passion leads her to ruin and to her own death; her tragedy is bound up with her rank and her feelings. In *Tartuffe*, by contrast, Elmire takes control of the situation. She has her husband hide under the table so that he can hear for himself as she draws Tartuffe into revealing his true intentions. Elmire stays calm and plans her actions wisely, and she is the one who exposes the hypocrite, not her husband.

In *The Way of the World*, the female lead, Millamant, also has power. In one famous scene she negotiates the terms of her marriage with Mirabell, listing the freedoms she insists on keeping as a wife. She refuses to give up being courted: "I'll be solicited to the very last; nay, and afterwards" (Congreve 4.1). And only once her conditions are accepted, she says, "I may by degrees dwindle into a wife." This shows a huge change in how women were represented on stage. Instead of being silent or only reacting to male characters, Elmire and Millamant make decisions and stand up for themselves. Both speak clearly and act with confidence, reflecting new ideas about freedom of speech and choice.

## The Influence of Commedia dell'Arte

One reason these kinds of characters appeared was the influence of commedia dell'arte, a type of Italian theatre built on stock roles like clever maids, foolish old men, and scheming lovers. As Eugène van Erven shows, this kind of popular theatre made fun of the upper classes and gave a voice to ordinary people. It helped inspire characters like Dorine, Foible, and Sir Wilfull Witwoud. Sir Wilfull, though seen as a country fool, often exposes how ridiculous the fashionable people around him are. He adds humour, but also shows that class does not equal intelligence.

These plays also responded to big social changes. Theatre became a space to question not just class, but also power and gender roles. It became a mirror in which people could see their own experiences and hear their own voices, and in which society could watch itself change.

## A World Without Servants: Phaedra

Compared with *Phaedra*, the difference is clear. In Racine's play, all the characters are nobles. The story is about private passion and shame, not about society or class. There are no clever servants, and the lower classes do not speak. As Paul Kuritz explains, Racine's characters seem noble but are "battling inescapable primitive passions" (212). Their problems are personal, not political, and they have little to do with the lives of most of the audience.

## Who Deserves to Be Heard?

*Tartuffe* and *The Way of the World* broke away from that style. They gave voice to people who were often ignored and marginalized. Servants, women, and the middle class became the characters who were smart, active, and central to the story. These plays show how theatre was changing just as society was. Instead of only glorifying kings and heroes, it began to ask: who really deserves to be heard?
