*A guide to trauma for anyone in a helping job*

If you work with people, you hear hard stories. Maybe you are a teacher, a nurse, a social worker, a librarian, a settlement worker, a caregiver, or the person at the front desk who everyone talks to. Some days you go home carrying stories that are not yours.

I know this feeling well. I work at a public library, and I coordinate a weekly English conversation group for newcomers. People share stories about leaving home, losing family, and starting over. This term I took a university course on trauma-informed librarianship, and for the first time I learned that what I was feeling has a name. I want to share what I learned in simple words, because I think many of us need it.

## So, what is trauma?

Trauma is not only about the bad thing that happened. It is about how that thing affected you. It is the lasting impact of something that felt overwhelming, scary, or deeply upsetting, something that shook your sense of safety or control. Two people can live through the same event, and one is fine while the other is still struggling months later. Neither one is doing it wrong.

Trauma is also very common. About 70% of people go through at least one traumatic event in their lifetime.

Here is the part most people don't know: you can be affected by trauma without it happening to you. When you listen to other people's painful stories again and again, some of that pain can stay with you. This has a few names: secondary trauma, vicarious trauma, or compassion fatigue. A big U.S. government guide on trauma-informed care calls it a normal occupational hazard for people who care for others. In other words, it is not a sign that you are weak. It is a sign that you care, and that caring has a cost.

## How do you know it's getting to you?

The signs usually creep in slowly, so they are easy to miss. Some show up in your body: you sleep badly, you get headaches or stomachaches, or you feel tired all the time. Some show up in your mood: you get triggered by small things, you snap at people you love, or you feel numb, anxious, or hopeless. Some show up in what you do: you pull away from friends and family, you eat a lot more or a lot less, you keep working nonstop, or you stop doing the things that used to make you happy. And some show up in how you think: you start to feel cynical, you see danger everywhere, or you feel like you have to fix everyone's problems by yourself.

For me, the first signs are that I get triggered faster than usual, my sleep gets messy, and I want to be alone in silence. I also stop taking care of myself. Your signs might look different. This is not a checklist for a diagnosis. It is about noticing your own pattern so you can catch it early.

## What can you do about it?

The same guide gives a simple way to think about self-care, called the ABCs: Awareness, Balance, and Connection.

**Awareness** means paying attention to your own needs and limits. Try a short check-in at the end of the day. How did I sleep? How did I eat? How quickly did I get upset today? If you see your warning signs for several days in a row, take it as a signal to slow down, not to push harder.

**Balance** means making room for rest and for things that fill you up, not only things that drain you. Learn to say no, and ask for help when the work piles up. Make a small break between work and home, like a short walk, a song in the car, or five quiet minutes before you walk in the door. Find something that calms your body. It might be cooking, gardening, prayer, music, making something with your hands, or a walk.

**Connection** means not carrying it all alone. Talk to a coworker who understands, a friend, or a support group. If your workplace has team meetings, it can help to talk about hard moments there too. And please think about talking to a counselor or therapist. Many workplaces offer free, private counseling through an employee assistance program. I am working on this one myself, because I am not someone who likes to share my problems. But I am learning that asking for help is part of taking care of yourself, not a failure.

Keep it small. A ten-minute walk counts. A few minutes of quiet counts. Small habits you can keep are better than big plans you drop after a week.

## When to get more help

If the signs last for weeks, if they make it hard to work, sleep, or take care of your family, or if you are using alcohol or other substances to cope, please reach out to a doctor or a mental health professional. And if you are ever in crisis or thinking about hurting yourself, please contact your local emergency number or a crisis line right away.

## One last thing

On an airplane, they always tell you to put your own oxygen mask on first before helping others. This is not selfish. If you care for other people for a living, taking care of yourself is how you keep being able to show up for them.
