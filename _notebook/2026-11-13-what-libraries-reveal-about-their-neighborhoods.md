We usually think of a public library as a place to borrow books. Spend a few afternoons in library branches across a large city, though, and you start to see something else: each branch is a mirror of the neighborhood around it.

For a recent research project, I studied eleven public library branches in a large, diverse city. I combined a full year of usage figures for each branch (how much was borrowed, how often people asked staff for help, and how many people attended in-person programs) with neighborhood data on income, education, immigration, renting, walkability and transit. Then I spent time inside the branches, simply watching how people used them. The results challenged some of my assumptions.

## What earlier research tells us

Researchers have long linked library use to the people who live nearby. Neighborhoods with more college graduates tend to borrow more (Japzon and Gong, 2005), and surveys of library users show clear differences in use by age, gender and education (Mushtaq and Arshad, 2022).

For immigrants, the library can be a place of support, but also of barriers: language, unfamiliar systems and digital tools, lack of time, and a history of libraries serving newcomers unequally (Naderi, 2024; Shuva, 2022). Economic conditions matter too. During the Great Recession, library visits rose sharply as people turned to free resources (Mabe, 2022), while long-term engagement tends to be strongest in economically stable communities (Skelly et al., 2015). Libraries in walkable, transit-connected neighborhoods are busier (Lenstra and Carlos, 2019), and in low-income areas they are often a lifeline for computers and internet access (Ly and Duck, 2010).

What is still rare is looking at all of these factors together, branch by branch, inside a single city. That is what I tried to do.

## The busiest libraries are not in the richest neighborhoods

The first surprise was that wealth does not predict library use. The most engaged branches, measured by borrowing, help requests and program attendance together, were in neighborhoods with middle incomes, relatively high education levels and large immigrant populations.

The branches in the wealthiest neighborhoods told a different story. Despite very high education levels, their overall engagement was modest, and one small branch in an affluent area recorded almost no borrowing and almost no questions at the desk, even though it still ran programs. Where people can easily afford home internet, books and paid activities, they seem to rely less on the public library.

## Where the programs fill the room

The clearest pattern of all was in program attendance. In immigrant-dense neighborhoods with moderate to high education, several branches welcomed more than 20,000 people to in-person programs in a single year. There, residents were not only borrowing books; they were using the library as a gathering place for learning, settling into a new country, and keeping children and teenagers busy and safe.

In wealthier neighborhoods with fewer immigrants, program attendance was noticeably lower. Even a branch that combined high income with a large immigrant population showed strong borrowing and many questions for staff, but more modest program numbers.

## Asking for help and borrowing go together

Branches where people asked staff more questions were also the branches where people borrowed more. Help at the desk is not a side service; it is often the door into the collection, especially for people who are new to the system, still learning the language, or uncomfortable with digital tools.

## What you see when you sit and watch

Numbers only tell part of the story. Watching the branches, four patterns stood out.

**After school, the youth areas fill up.** In immigrant-dense, lower-income neighborhoods, the libraries stay busy all day, and after school large groups of children gather around the youth areas, computers and program rooms. Some attend structured programs; others drop in for a snack or to be with friends. Teenagers crowd around video-game stations and shared computers, a sign that many don't have these resources at home.

**The computers are never free.** In immigrant-dense and renter-heavy areas, public computers are in constant use. People wait in line and rotate between job applications, documents, homework and messages, and many ask for help with printing, scanning or navigating online forms.

**In affluent neighborhoods, it is quiet.** In higher-income areas, patrons browse on their own, pick up holds, or settle into a reading chair for a long while. Youth spaces are calmer, programs draw smaller groups, and computers often sit empty even at busy times.

**Downtown, people come and go.** In a walkable, renter-dominated downtown neighborhood, the library is a place of constant short visits: using the Wi-Fi, printing a page, asking a quick question, or simply resting for a few minutes. Seats and computers change hands all day.

## One library, many roles

Put together, the numbers and the observations say the same thing: public libraries do not play a single, uniform role across a city. They adapt to the neighborhoods around them.

Where families face cramped housing, financial pressure or the challenges of settling in a new country, the library becomes a multi-purpose community anchor: a study room, an office, a youth center and a help desk all at once. In wealthier neighborhoods, it becomes something more individual and resource-based, a place to borrow and to read.

This matters for how we fund and judge libraries. If we measure a branch only by how many books it lends, we miss most of the work it does. The libraries that matter most to their communities are often the ones where the computers are always busy, the program rooms are full, and the line at the help desk never quite disappears.
